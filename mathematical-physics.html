Mathematical physics

Exact structures in low-dimensional gravity and gauge theory: topological recursion and spectral curves, and operator-algebraic approaches to quantum field theory.

The area

Two strands, with different flavours but a shared appeal — both replace approximation with structure.

The first is the Eynard–Orantin topological recursion, which generates the full genus expansion of a matrix model from a single spectral curve. It connects Weil–Petersson volumes of moduli space, JT gravity, and the double-scaled SYK model, and the q-deformed setting brings in quantum-group structure while preserving enough of the recursion to remain computable. Supersymmetric generalizations add their own subtleties at the ramification points.

The second is algebraic QFT, where the primary object is the net of local observable algebras rather than fields. That framing makes questions about symmetry and locality precise in a way Lagrangian methods do not — including when scale invariance implies conformal invariance, and what completeness conditions on the net are needed to say so.

Questions I'm interested in

  • What does the spectral curve encode? Given a curve, recovering the matrix model potential — and knowing when that recovery is unique — is a recurring practical question.
  • How do discrete volumes generalize under q-deformation? Okuyama-style discrete volumes have a clean structure in the undeformed case; the deformed and supersymmetric cases do not always follow suit, and the obstructions are informative.
  • How does topological recursion apply to Seiberg–Witten curves? The distinction between a Seiberg–Witten curve and a spectral curve in the Eynard–Orantin sense matters here, as do admissibility and non-simple ramification.
  • When does scale invariance imply conformal invariance? The question is classical, but the operator-algebraic formulation makes the hypotheses explicit rather than implicit.
  • What is the algebraic content of confinement? Type III factors, subfactor indices, and modular theory give a vocabulary for gauge-theoretic questions that is not obviously the natural one — which is part of what makes it worth trying.
